XPRS

eXtended Packet Radio System

Every message you send today goes through somebody's tower, somebody's server and somebody's account. When any of them is down, busy or switched off, you are not talking to anyone.

XPRS is a way for devices to talk without any of that. Small text messages, carried by whatever radio is at hand and passed along by whoever hears them. No towers, no accounts, no phone number, no company in the middle.

Why you would use it

  • It works when the rest does not. A power cut, a storm, a jammed network, a boat, a valley with no coverage. If someone is in range, you can reach them.
  • Nobody can switch it off. There is no server to seize, no account to suspend and no bill. The network is the people running it.
  • Your identity is yours. A key made on your device that nobody issues and nobody can revoke. Messages are signed with it, so a name on the air can be checked rather than trusted.
  • It runs on what you already have. Bluetooth in your phone, a twenty-euro LoRa board, an old handheld, the WiFi in the room, or the internet when there is one.
  • You can read it yourself. Plain text you can print and understand - no binary format, nothing to reverse engineer.
  • It does not rot. Fields nobody knows are skipped instead of rejected, so an old device keeps working with a new one for years.

What a message looks like

At most 250 bytes, so it fits one cheap radio transmission:

A message to a friend:

t:message f:X1QZ3N d:X1RD89 ts:2026-08-18_09:15:00 m:arrived at the marina, see you at the cafe

A call for help, relayed further than anything else:

t:sos f:X1QZ3N pos:38.7223,-9.1393 kind:medical ts:2026-08-18_09:20:00 m:broken leg, cannot walk

The same messages travel LoRa, Bluetooth, WiFi, ham radio bands, wired networks and the internet, unchanged. And because it is a flat list of names and values, it is already JSON if your program prefers that:

{"t":"message","f":"X1QZ3N","d":"X1RD89",
 "ts":"2026-08-18_09:15:00","m":"arrived at the marina"}

Values stay text, because the unit is part of the value - temp:14.2C is "14.2C", never 14.2. Section 4.10 has the conversion both ways.

Sharing files, BitTorrent style

A file is shared the way BitTorrent shares one: broken into pieces, and anyone who has a piece can pass it on. You do not download from a server, you collect it from whoever is nearby and has part of it.

Asking who has a photo:

t:request f:X1QZ3N ts:2026-08-08_14:26:40 q:have file:nYxKzGm4vT2pQ8dW5jR7cL0aFbNs9hUe3oXiC6EkM1w.jpg

Two stations answer. One has all of it, the other only 412 pieces out of 900 - and it can still send you those 412:

t:result f:X3RLY7 d:X1QZ3N ts:2026-08-08_14:26:41 r:17d873 s:have have:full size:240kB
t:result f:X3RLY7 d:X1QZ3N ts:2026-08-08_14:26:41 r:17d873 s:have have:412/900 size:58MB

Nobody has to finish a download before being useful to the next person. The long name is the file's fingerprint, so every piece is checked as it arrives and a file assembled from six strangers is exactly the file that was published.

Private messages

You can send a message that only the person it is addressed to can read. Everyone else sees that a message went from someone to someone, and when - but not a word of what it says:

t:message f:X1QZ3N d:X1RD89 ts:2026-08-08_14:26:40 x:pQ4m9xT2vB8kR sig:<60 characters>

The sender, the recipient and the time stay in the open on purpose. A relay in between has to see those to pass the message along, or to hold it until the recipient comes back in range. It never needs to see the content.

Or hide only parts of one, the way a released document has passages blacked out:

meet ((Max)) at ((pier2))   is sent as   meet ███ at █████

Anyone with the passphrase reads it whole; everyone else still gets a useful message. The same trick blurs a position instead of hiding it - public to about ten kilometres, exact with the passphrase.

What a device can be

One device usually does several at once, and you decide which. A station is you, sending and receiving. A relay repeats what it hears so it travels further. A carrier holds messages for people out of range until they return. A gateway bridges radio and internet. An indexer keeps an archive for the stations that asked it to. Nobody assigns these - you choose.

If you already use APRS

XPRS is APRS carried forward. The idea is unchanged - small text packets, heard by anyone, repeated by volunteers - with three decades of lessons applied.

  • Identity is proven. Packets are signed with a key the station holds, and a licensed callsign can be bound to that key.
  • Delivery is tracked. Stations hold mail for operators who are away, and hand it over against a signed receipt - so you can ask for what you missed instead of losing it.
  • No central server. APRS routes through one internet backbone. In XPRS each station picks its own archives, and they exchange directories instead of copying each other's traffic.
  • Files and privacy. Neither existed before: whole files move over the air, and messages can be sealed off it.

Do you need a licence?

Not for most of it. LoRa and Bluetooth in the ISM bands, WiFi and the internet are licence-free, and your identity is a key your device makes - nothing to register, nobody to ask.

On amateur bands the amateur rules apply as always: plain text, your government-issued callsign, no encryption. XPRS respects that line rather than blurring it - sealed messages and self-made callsigns never go onto amateur spectrum.

Running it

  • xprs-dev/xprs-flutter the app for phones and desktops. Released for Android, Linux and Windows.
  • xprs-dev/xprs-esp32 firmware in C that turns a cheap board into a station, repeater and mailbox: T-Dongle-S3, M5Stack Core, Heltec LoRa, kv4p and others.
  • xprs-dev/reticulum-dart the shared networking library: mesh transport, a DHT, file sharing and the signature scheme.
  • xprs-dev/wapps the small applications that run inside the app, and the catalog it installs them from.

Writing your own

The format is small enough to implement in an afternoon, and fully specified.

  • XPRS.md the specification, edition XPRS-2026. Every section links on its own, as XPRS.md#3-callsigns.
  • xprs_corpus.json the conformance corpus: every example in the document with its byte count and identifier. Replay them exactly and you have read the format right.
  • github.com/xprs-dev/spec the specification repository

Credits

XPRS is built on work that came before it, and most of what makes it possible was designed by other people.

  • Reticulum - Mark Qvist the cryptography-first networking stack XPRS uses to cross the mesh and the internet.
  • NOSTR - fiatjaf the event and relay model, and the keypair a station's identity is built on.
  • APRS - Bob Bruninga, WB4APR (SK) the original: small text packets, heard by anyone, repeated by volunteers. Begun in 1982. Everything here continues that idea.
  • APRS-IS - Steve Dimse, K4HG, and the team that followed APRServe, the first APRS-IS server, in 1997, and the servers and operators that have carried APRS across the internet ever since.
  • XPRS - Max Brito the founding implementation: the specification, the codecs, and the applications that speak it.

XPRS operates alongside APRS and respects its rules. Traffic from self-generated callsigns is never sent onto amateur infrastructure. Encrypted traffic is never sent on amateur bands.
