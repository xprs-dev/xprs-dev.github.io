XPRS

eXtended Packet Radio System

XPRS extends and modernizes APRS. The core idea stays the same: small text packets that anyone can hear and volunteers repeat across the network. Everything else was rebuilt with the lessons of three decades in the field.

A packet is at most 250 bytes of key:value text, readable without a decoder:

A message to a friend:

t:message f:X1QZ3N d:X1RD89 ts:2026-08-18_09:15:00 m:arrived at the marina, see you at the cafe

A call for help, relayed further than anything else:

t:sos f:X1QZ3N pos:38.7223,-9.1393 kind:medical ts:2026-08-18_09:20:00 m:broken leg, cannot walk

Back from four days at sea, asking a station for what was missed:

t:command f:X1BOA3 d:X3RLY7 ts:2026-08-18_09:45:00 cmd:history since:2026-08-14_00:00:00

A person can read every one of them and so can a five-line parser. The same packets travel LoRa, Bluetooth, WiFi, HF/VHF/UHF, wired networks and the internet.

A flat list of key and value is a JSON object already. Split each field on its first colon and you are done - no schema, nothing to know about the packet type:

{"t":"message","f":"X1QZ3N","d":"X1RD89",
 "ts":"2026-08-18_09:15:00","m":"arrived at the marina"}

Values stay strings, because the unit is part of the value: temp:14.2C is "14.2C", never 14.2. Section 4.10 has both directions.

What changed from APRS

  • Identity is proven. Packets are signed with a key the station holds, and a licensed callsign can be bound to that key.
  • Delivery is tracked. Stations carry mail for absent peers against signed receipts, and a station that was away can ask for what it missed.
  • No central server. Where APRS routes through one internet backbone, XPRS uses indexers each station chooses, exchanging directories instead of copying each other's traffic.
  • Room to grow. Every value carries its unit, unknown keys are skipped without error, and new fields never break old readers.

Sharing files, BitTorrent style

A file is shared the way BitTorrent shares one: broken into pieces, and anyone who has a piece can pass it on. You do not download from a server, you collect it from whoever is nearby and has part of it.

Asking who has a photo:

t:request f:X1QZ3N ts:2026-08-08_14:26:40 q:have file:nYxKzGm4vT2pQ8dW5jR7cL0aFbNs9hUe3oXiC6EkM1w.jpg

Two stations answer. One has all of it, the other only 412 pieces out of 900 - and it can still send you those 412:

t:result f:X3RLY7 d:X1QZ3N ts:2026-08-08_14:26:41 r:17d873 s:have have:full size:240kB
t:result f:X3RLY7 d:X1QZ3N ts:2026-08-08_14:26:41 r:17d873 s:have have:412/900 size:58MB

Nobody has to finish a download before being useful to the next person. The long name in the request is the file's fingerprint, so every piece is checked as it arrives and a file assembled from six strangers is exactly the file that was published. Whole folders work the same way, and re-sharing a changed folder only moves what actually changed.

Private messages

You can send a message that only the person it is addressed to can read. Everyone else sees that a message went from someone to someone, and when - but not a word of what it says:

t:message f:X1QZ3N d:X1RD89 ts:2026-08-08_14:26:40 x:pQ4m9xT2vB8kR sig:<60 characters>

The sender, the recipient and the time stay in the open on purpose. A relay in between has to see those to pass the message along, or to hold it until the recipient comes back in range. It never needs to see the content.

You can also hide only parts of a message, the way a released document has passages blacked out. The author marks the secrets, and everyone sees where the gaps are and how long they are:

meet ((Max)) at ((pier2))   is sent as   meet ███ at █████

Anyone with the passphrase reads the whole thing. Everyone else still gets a useful message. The same trick blurs a position rather than hiding it: the public version stays accurate to about ten kilometres, and the passphrase recovers the exact spot.

None of this is used on amateur radio bands, where the rules require plain text.

Roles in the network

All of them run on ordinary devices, and one device usually plays several. A station speaks under a callsign; a relay repeats what it hears; a carrier holds mail for absent stations; a gateway bridges radio and internet; an indexer archives the publications of the stations that chose it. No role is assigned by an authority - operators choose what their devices do, and set their own limits.

Radio frequencies usage

No licence is needed on licence-free spectrum: LoRa and Bluetooth in the ISM bands, WiFi and the internet. Identity comes from a keypair generated on the device. On amateur bands XPRS operates under amateur rules: plain text, a government-issued callsign, no ciphertext.

Specification

  • XPRS.md the full specification, edition XPRS-2026. Every section is linkable on its own, as XPRS.md#3-callsigns.
  • xprs_corpus.json the conformance corpus: every worked example as a wire with its byte count and identifier. Replay them byte-exact and you read the format.
  • github.com/xprs-dev/spec the specification repository

Reference implementations

  • xprs-dev/xprs-flutter phones and desktops, in Dart and Flutter: codec, signing, store-and-forward, the heard-traffic archive and the transports. Released for Android, Linux and Windows.
  • xprs-dev/xprs-esp32 firmware in C: a board becomes a station, digipeater and mailbox. T-Dongle-S3, M5Stack Core, Heltec LoRa, kv4p and others, over BLE5, ESP-NOW, WiFi and LoRa.
  • xprs-dev/reticulum-dart the shared Dart networking library. Reticulum mesh transport, a DHT, content-addressed file sharing and the signature scheme.
  • xprs-dev/wapps the applications that run inside the host, compiled to WebAssembly, and the catalog the launcher installs from.

Credits

XPRS is built on work that came before it, and most of what makes it possible was designed by other people.

  • Reticulum - Mark Qvist the cryptography-first networking stack XPRS uses to cross the mesh and the internet. Addresses derived from keys, no central authority, and one protocol over LoRa, packet radio, WiFi or TCP/IP.
  • NOSTR - fiatjaf the event and relay model, and the secp256k1 keypair a station's identity is built on. Signed events over simple relays, 2020.
  • APRS - Bob Bruninga, WB4APR (SK) the original: small text packets, heard by anyone, repeated by volunteers. Begun in 1982, named APRS in 1992. Everything in this format is a continuation of that idea.
  • APRS-IS - Steve Dimse, K4HG, and the team that followed APRServe, the first APRS-IS server, in 1997, and the tier of servers and operators that has carried APRS traffic across the internet ever since - including javAPRSSrvr by Pete Loveall, AE5PL.
  • XPRS - Max Brito the founding implementation: the specification, the Dart and C codecs, and the applications that speak it.

XPRS operates alongside APRS and respects its rules. Traffic from self-generated callsigns is never sent onto amateur infrastructure. Encrypted traffic is never sent on amateur bands.
